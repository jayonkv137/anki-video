## 1 · THE METHOD — reverse scenario generation
**Never pick a setting and force a grammar point into it.** That is the failure mode that produces thin, unnatural scenes built around a rule. Work backwards instead:

> **① Take the target structure → ② find the real-world situation that naturally and frequently *demands* it → ③ write the scene around that situation.**

*Worked example (from the source research): the target is two-way prepositions with static location. The situation that genuinely demands them is **moving into a room and saying where the furniture goes**. The scene is therefore a character moving in — and the grammar appears dozens of times without anyone teaching it.*

**The acceptance test — apply it before writing a line:**
> *Would a real person in this situation naturally produce this structure more than once, without being asked to?*

If no, **the situation is wrong. Change the situation, never bend the grammar.** A scene where someone demonstrates a rule is a lesson wearing a costume; a scene where the situation demands the rule is a story that happens to teach.

**Why this works:** when the structure is required by what is happening, the learner meets it as *language being used* rather than *a rule being shown* — repeatedly, in context, with the meaning visible on screen.

## 2 · THE THREE INPUTS
Every scene is derived from exactly three things:
1. **The lesson atoms** — what this block must teach (`CURRICULUM`).
2. **The story so far** — where these characters are, what has happened, what is unresolved (`UNIVERSE_STATE` + `SHOW_BIBLE` §11).
3. **The cast** — who is available, whose turn it is (`SHOW_BIBLE` §8).

**When they pull against each other**, resolve in this order:
- **The atoms are fixed** — the block teaches what the curriculum says.
- **The situation is free** — if the natural situation for this structure doesn't fit where the story currently is, *move the story to it* (characters travel, time passes, someone visits) rather than forcing an unnatural situation.
- **The cast can flex** — if the lead's rotation doesn't suit the situation, a different character leads and rotation rebalances later.
- **Never compromise the atom** to protect a story idea. The lesson is the reason the episode exists.

## 3 · THE LOAD-BALANCE LAW
A viewer's working memory is finite and shared: `total load = linguistic + narrative`. Therefore the two must move **inversely**.

| When the block is… | The other side must be… |
|---|---|
| **Introducing new grammar** | plot **simple**, location **familiar**, few characters, nothing surprising structurally |
| **A big story beat** (a reveal, a reunion, a decision) | language **already acquired** — no new structure competes with the drama |

This is a planning rule, not a style note: **check it before writing.** A module that introduces a hard structure should not also carry the season's biggest turn. If the plan does both, split them across blocks.

## 4 · ENGINES — devices that manufacture repetition without repeating
Long-running language series use a structural device that makes natural repetition inevitable. Three proven ones, available as tools:
- **The mystery** *(Destinos)* — an unresolved question forces characters to summarise what they know for each new person they meet. Recaps become story, and repetition becomes free.
- **The misunderstanding** *(Extr@)* — a character misreads a situation, others correct and clarify, and the same structures cycle through the correction.
- **The navigation journey** *(Nicos Weg)* — the protagonist's practical needs *are* the syllabus: what they must accomplish is what the learner must be able to say.

**Our own engine: ⧖ OPEN.** The series will develop its own device as the universe is built. Until then, an engine may be borrowed for a single episode when it fits; none is the show's standing mechanism. When ours emerges, it is recorded here.

## 5 · SCENE CRAFT
- **The camera explains the dialogue.** The visual is not decoration — it is the translation layer. Blocking, gesture, distance between characters, what someone is holding and where they look must make the meaning of a line guessable *without the German*. A line whose meaning isn't visible on screen has failed.
- **Tie the target structure to a visible action.** The structure and the thing happening must be the same event. If a character says *"man darf hier nicht parken"*, something in frame is being prevented.
- **Write for content words.** Learners fasten onto nouns and verbs and skim past function words. Build scenes where the concrete things being named are physically present and handled.
- **Humour lives in the situation, never in the words.** Wordplay is invisible to a learner and emotional-memory boosts are weaker in a second language — so the funny thing must be *what is happening*, legible to someone who understands none of the dialogue. Absurdity aids memory only when it is genuinely funny; strange-but-unfunny is just noise.
- **Visually comic, linguistically plain.** The situation may be as absurd as you like; the sentences stay ordinary.
- **One legible environment per episode.** A simple, stable setting caps the vocabulary the viewer must hold and keeps the content schema free for language.
- **Dialogue is the universal solvent.** Anything unfilmable — an abstract idea, a concept, a rule — becomes filmable the moment a character *says* it in a situation that motivates saying it.
- **Keep proper nouns stable.** Unfamiliar names disrupt listening fluency exactly like unknown vocabulary. Reuse the cast and the established places; introduce a new name only when it matters, and repeat it within the episode.

## 6 · THE 30-SECOND BLOCK
Every episode is one ~30-second block (`CURRICULUM` §1). Its anatomy:
- **The hook (first seconds).** An image that reads with the **sound off and no prior context**. It is the situation's absurdity made visible — never a title, never a question on screen.
- **The body.** The situation plays out and the target structure appears in the course of it, more than once, in different mouths or different moments.
- **The button.** End on a turn — a reversal, a beat of character, an unresolved look. **Not** a summary and not a resolution.
- **Standalone rule.** A viewer arriving with no context must not feel lost. Every block gives enough situation to stand alone, even while it advances a serial arc. Do not slice one continuous scene into fragments that only work in sequence.
- **Dialogue budget.** Roughly ten to twelve short lines with rapid turn-taking — conversation, not monologue. Exact word and pace ceilings live in `PEDAGOGY.md`.

## 7 · OPTIONAL SHAPES (offers, never templates)
When a scene needs a spine, these are available. The agent may **suggest** one; it never imposes one, and a scene that works without any of them is not deficient.
- **The escalation shape:** base reality → the first unusual thing → framing ("if this is true, then…") → escalation by logical doubling → the button.
- **Recurring situation types** that have proven productive in this world: a rule being enforced on someone · a ritual performed without explanation · an expectation quietly failed · a system being navigated. Each implies a different scene shape and a different grammar family.

### 7.1 · The narrative episode — when the story is the priority
Some episodes carry no new language: the Season-0 arrivals, and occasionally one inside a lesson that plants a thread, changes a relationship, or pays something off. They are declared in the lesson plan (`format: narrative | season_zero`) and **must state what they move** — a scene that moves nothing is decoration, however good it looks.

**What opens up — and it is visual, never verbal:**
- **One legible environment may be broken** (§10.3) when the story genuinely crosses worlds — a portal sequence is two places by design.
- **The button may become a reveal, a cliffhanger, or a held image** instead of a turn (§6).
- **The optional shapes (§7) are set aside.** This is where invention belongs.

**What does not open up:** the model's real limits (`TREATMENT` §8, §8.3 — declaring an episode narrative buys no extra cuts, and eighteen cuts in fifteen seconds fails for a story reason too) · identity, the material laws and the naming law · comprehensibility (`PEDAGOGY` §2.1 — the language gets *less*, not more) · the hook readable muted, which here is the entire job.

> **When the constraints relax, judgement must tighten.** In a teaching episode the ceilings catch a great deal of weak work. Here nothing catches it except taste — so this is the mode in which an agreeable agent is most dangerous. The right behaviour is more options and bolder ones, critique aimed at whether the beat is *earned* rather than whether a rule was met, and a plain refusal to call a scene finished because it is pretty.

## 8 · ARC & CONTINUITY
- **Continuity is pedagogy, not bookkeeping.** A stable cast, world and situation keep the viewer's *content schema* fixed, which frees working memory for language. Every time we reuse a place, a relationship or a running joke, comprehension gets cheaper.
- **A module advances the journey.** Each module is a story-arc unit; by its end something about the characters' situation has moved. The curriculum supplies the language; the arc supplies the reason to watch the next one.
- **A lesson's episodes share a through-line** (recorded in its plan) and each may reference the ones before it — but **the standalone rule (§6) outranks the arc**: an episode may reference an earlier one, never *require* it, because most viewers will see exactly one.
- **Plant and pay off.** An object, a person or an unresolved line introduced in one module should return. Planted threads are recorded in state; paying one off is always a stronger option than inventing something new.
- **The story so far is a creative asset.** Ask what is already true before inventing: an established location costs nothing, an established relationship carries meaning for free, and an unresolved thread is a scene waiting to happen.
- **Never contradict established facts.** If a new idea conflicts with what an episode already established, either the idea changes or the conflict is raised for a human decision (`SHOW_BIBLE` §15.4). Never silently overwrite.

## 9 · VARIETY & FAILURE MODES
### 9.1 Variety (the anti-slop engine)
Language models collapse toward the statistically average trope; across 170 episodes that produces a show that feels like one episode repeated. Defences:
- **Rotate the pairings.** Four characters make six pairs, each with a distinct friction. Track which have been used.
- **Rotate the situation type and the thematic domain** — don't run three rule-enforcement scenes in a row.
- **Keep a coverage log** of used situations, locations, stereotypes and comedic angles.
- **Anchor on the human.** Jayon's own observation or idea, injected into the scenario, is the most reliable disruptor of the average — an agent's unprompted invention is the least.
- **Use a lateral constraint when a scene feels generic** — an arbitrary restriction ("the conflict is over an object smaller than a coin", "one character controls the scene through silence") forces a non-obvious solution.
- **Ban lazy thematic vocabulary** in dialogue — the words that signal a machine reaching for the nearest cliché about Germany.

### 9.2 The five ways this goes wrong
| Failure | What it looks like | Guard |
|---|---|---|
| **The didactic trap** | a character explains grammar or vocabulary; the fourth wall breaks | never explain language *in* the fiction — the teaching is structural, never spoken |
| **Explaining the stereotype** | dialogue names or justifies the cultural behaviour | it is scenography and situation only (`SHOW_BIBLE` §12.4) |
| **Unfilmable action drift** | crowds, complex hand work, fast movement, physical contact | keep the action inside what the model can render (`TREATMENT` §8) |
| **Persona bleed** | all four start sounding the same | re-read the character's section before writing their lines; if a line could be swapped between two characters unchanged, rewrite it |
| **Dialogue inflation** | the block runs long and loops | conversation, not monologue; cut anything that repeats a beat already landed |

## 10 · HARD vs SOFT
**HARD — checked and blocked:**
1. No character explains grammar, vocabulary, or the lesson. No fourth wall.
2. The stereotype is never named or explained in dialogue.
3. One legible environment per episode.
4. The hook reads with the sound off.
5. Scene density and action stay inside model limits (`TREATMENT` §8).
6. Nothing contradicts established canon facts.

**SOFT — flagged, advisory, never blocking:** the method, the acceptance test, the load-balance law, engines, all scene craft, block anatomy, the optional shapes, arc guidance, and every variety lever. These exist so the agent can **suggest better** — not so a writer can be told no.
